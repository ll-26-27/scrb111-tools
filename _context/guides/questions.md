# Writing follow-up questions about a student's video

*Working draft, November 24, 2026. Adapted from Marlon's first draft in `video-assignment-tools` for SCRB 111. This file is the professor's to rewrite: the questions skill reads all of it every time, so changing a sentence here changes the questions.*

The videos are students presenting a piece of scientific work, usually a section of a paper read for the course, often from a visual they made themselves. The questions are for the professor to ask each student afterwards: in conversation, in written feedback, or as a prompt for the student's own reflection. They open a conversation; they aren't a verdict. A good set makes the student want to say more about the science and helps them see their own explanation more clearly.

## What makes a good question

- **Grounded in a moment.** Tie most questions to a specific time, sentence, or thing on screen, and quote or describe it: "At 01:42 you said the knockout 'basically proves' the pathway is required. What would the authors have had to see to conclude the opposite?" A question that could be asked of any video is weak.
- **Open.** It can't be answered yes or no, and it doesn't hint at the answer you want.
- **Curious, not corrective.** If something sounds wrong or thin, ask the student to walk through it rather than announcing the error: "Take me through how the arrow from A to B in your drawing follows from the figure." Put the suspected error in `why`, for the professor.
- **One step past what was said.** The best questions ask for the next thing: the control that isn't mentioned, the alternative explanation, what the result predicts, how the method limits the claim. Don't ask what the video already answers.
- **One thing at a time.** No two-part questions.
- **Plain words,** short enough to say aloud. Use the student's own terms for things where they are correct.

## Kinds of question (`kind`)

Aim for a mix; not every kind is needed every time.

- `understanding`: a concept, mechanism, or claim the student stated. Ask them to explain it another way, define a term they used, or connect two things they said.
- `evidence`: what supports a claim: which experiment, which figure, which control, what the data would look like if it were otherwise.
- `visual`: a choice in the visual they made or showed: what an arrow, colour, or layout stands for, what was left out, what the drawing made easier or harder to explain.
- `process`: how they got there: what was hard to understand in the paper, what changed between first reading and presenting, how they decided what to include.
- `extension`: what follows: the next experiment, a limitation, a link to something else in the course, what they would ask the authors.

## Observations

`observations` are moments the professor might want to watch before talking with the student: an explanation that lands, a claim that goes past the evidence, a term used loosely, a place where the visual carries the explanation. Say what is there, specifically, with its time. They're for the professor, so they can be more direct than the questions, but they describe; they don't grade.

## How many

Five to eight questions per video and up to six observations. Fewer, better questions beat a long list.

## Stay inside the evidence

- You have a transcript and some stills, not the video. If a question depends on what happens between frames, say so in `why` or put it in `uncertain`.
- The transcriber mishears names, gene and protein names, and quiet speech, and can invent words during silence. If a technical term looks wrong, don't build a question on it; list it in `uncertain` with its time.
- Don't assert that the student got the science wrong unless the course materials in `_context/` let you check it. Otherwise ask.
- If there is little or no speech, lean on the stills and say the transcript was thin.

## Leave out

- Grades, scores, rankings, or comparisons with other students.
- Praise that isn't specific, and compliments phrased as questions.
- Guesses about the student's personal life, feelings, or identity.
- The student's name, unless the professor asks for it.
