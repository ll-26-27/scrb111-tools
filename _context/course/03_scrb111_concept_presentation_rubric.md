# SCRB 111 Concept Presentation Rubric

Instead of a final exam, students work in pairs and will use their knowledge of regenerative biology to engage a broad, public audience in a current topic in stem cell and/or regenerative biology in a ~5-minute video recorded presentation.

## Audience

Your presentation should be targeted to the general public. Avoid scientific jargon or be careful to explicitly define it. Remember an important part of the evaluation will also be whether you have explained your topic’s importance (problem), background, and future directions well.

## Timing

### Set-up

We expect the first 1-2 minutes will focus on laying out why the audience should care about this topic. For example:

• If you’re proposing a stem-cell based treatment, or a treatment that genetically modifies endogenous cells in the body, who will benefit? How many people? Is it specially tailored to certain versions of the disease? Certain other attributes of patient population? Shortcomings of previous treatments and need for innovation?

• If you’re proposing to use stem cell technology to develop a consumer product, what is its use? Will it replace something else, and why is it better?

• If you’re proposing to genetically modify a crop or a wild animal population, why is this a necessary or important innovation? What is the problem with status quo?

### Meaty section

We expect 2-3 minutes to focus on how this actually works. For example:

• Stem-cell based treatment: Where do the cells originate from? Are they amplified in the lab? Are they genetically modified or otherwise treated transiently? How is quality controlled? How are they delivered, and how many treatments? How are outcomes assessed? What are the risks?

• Genetically modifying endogenous cells in a patient: What exact target cells? How does it work? How many treatments? Risks? Outcomes?

• Products that humans use: What are the nuts and bolts of making this? Cost versus currently- available alternatives? Hidden ingredients that might surprise the average consumer? Any deleterious possible effects of using/consuming/etc.?

• Genetically modifying other organisms for human benefit: Philosophically, why undertake this initiative? How does it help humans and/or other creatures? How could it hurt them? How might it play out in the very long term?

### Outlook

Address what problems remain, opportunities for optimization, long-term prospects.

## Grading standards

### A range

A presentation in the A range proposes an important and interesting unsolved question that can be succinctly approached with the proposed experiments.

• The topic and background material should be explained in an engaging way that pays homage to the work of others while still pointing out where current understanding falls short.

• Problem: Why other methods or strategies fall short—clearly explained.

• Meaty part: The main explanatory section is clearly and accurately communicated. There are no obviously missing topics/holes.

• Group participation: All group members should have screen time and speaking time.

• Style: Overall, the presentation should be fun to watch and the audience should learn something.

### B range

A presentation in the B range proposes an interesting unsolved question that can be approached with the proposed experiments, but it has one or more issues that do not elevate it to the A range.

• The topic and background material may strike at an interesting concept but fall short of clearly laying out why this subject matter is so compelling or what its relevance is.

• Problem: Mostly explained, but not completely clear why there needs to be another option, more innovation, etc.

• Meaty part: The presenters may not have clearly identified the limitations or they may identify them but inadequately engage them. There may be gaps in explanations of how things work.

• Group participation: All group members should have screen time and speaking time. In a B-range presentation, while all members speak on screen, one member might be monopolizing nearly all of the conversation.

• Style: Overall, the presentation should still be fun to watch and the audience should learn something. Unlike an A-range presentation, the audience might be left wondering if they got the whole point of the presentation.

### C range

A presentation in the C range still identifies a viable topic, though the explanations might not be the best ones to tackle the question, and it has one or more issues that do not elevate it to the B range.

• The topic may be variably interesting depending on the audience, but the presenters might not have given enough thought to how to spark intense interest. The background material may be presented, but it may be insufficient to understand the existing work enough to convincingly support the rest of the presentation.

• Problem may not be clearly stated. The need for innovation is not obvious.

• Meaty part: Typical considerations for the technology may not be presented. The expected challenges and outcomes might not be adequately discussed. Inadequate identification of exciting future directions.

• Group participation: All group members have screen time and speaking time. In a C-range presentation, while all members speak on screen, likely one member monopolizes nearly all of the conversation.

• Style: Overall, the presentation might still be fun to watch and the audience might still learn something. Unlike an A- or B-range presentation, the audience may be left with the primary emotion of confusion rather than understanding. The viewing audience may note a palpable amount of clumsiness in aspects such as transitions, which probably indicates the presenters did not thoroughly plan the project as well as they should have. There could also be a sizeable disconnect between the general public audience and the level of sophistication of the explanations provided in all categories.

### D-F range

A presentation in the D-F range means that the presenters decided they did not care to try and fulfill the assignment in a serious way.
